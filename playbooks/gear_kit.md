# Building a Travel Gear Kit

**What this playbook claims:** the reasoning behind gear picks for a long-term travel kit — footwear, shells, base layers, power, network hardware, and EDC — biased toward buy-it-once durability ("BIFL," buy it for life) over replacing cheap gear every year or two. Pair this with [[clothing_capsule]] for the wearable-matrix side and [[gear_intake_workflow]] for researching any slot you haven't decided on yet.

## Footwear

**Primary shoe.** Pick one shoe that covers the most modes — daily wear, light athletic use, travel days — and accept it won't do everything. A minimalist, wide-toe-box shoe (Altra and Vivobarefoot both make common reference points in this category) typically uses cemented-and-stitched construction with replaceable laces; sole life runs roughly 1,200–2,000 km depending on terrain, and while resoling isn't commercially supported on most of these, the uppers usually outlast the soles by a wide margin. Call that BIFL-adjacent rather than BIFL-pure — it won't last forever, but it won't fail early either — and favor a black or dark colorway, which blends better across regions than a light one.

**Secondary shoe.** A single do-everything shoe won't cover formal-adjacent or fashion-conscious urban contexts. Carry a clean dark sneaker or a dark Chelsea boot as a second pair, and compare candidates on durability, resoleability, and how well the silhouette actually reads in the cities you'll spend time in.

## Shells and outerwear

**Hard shell.** Favor a shell from a brand that's excellent but not globally recognized as a status symbol — the goal is "obviously good quality," not "obviously expensive." Look for a 3-layer waterproof-breathable membrane, taped seams, and quality water-resistant zippers (YKK Aquaguard or equivalent). With the DWR (durable water repellent) coating reapplied every 12–18 months, a shell built to that spec typically has a field life of 8–10 years. Confirm there's a real repair pathway — the brand's own service network or a competent third-party repair shop — before you commit; a shell with no repair path just becomes a replacement purchase in year five instead of year ten. Stick to dark colorways (black, dark navy, charcoal), and treat any hardshell as pack-only gear in tropical or hot, dry climates, where carrying one reads oddly regardless of brand.

**Packable / wind shell.** A second, lighter shell that compresses to fist-size covers daily weather insurance without hardshell bulk. Worth comparing: Patagonia Houdini, Black Diamond Distance Wind, and any strong domestic outdoor brand if you're based somewhere that has one — a home-market brand often reads as more neutral locally than an imported name does.

## Merino base and mid-layer

Merino wins the base/mid-layer slot for three reasons: it resists odor across multi-day wear without washing, it thermoregulates across a wider range than synthetics, and it dries fast. Quality merino holds up 5–10 years if washed cold and air-dried.

Brand weight and durability vary more than the marketing suggests — worth comparing before committing to one:

| Brand | Weight class | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Wool&Prince | Heavyweight (~190 gsm) | Longest reported field life; higher price |
| Unbound Merino | Midweight (~175 gsm) | Price/durability middle ground |
| Smartwool | Midweight | Wide availability; low novelty — a plus for blending in |
| Icebreaker | Premium | Supply-chain transparency; slightly more pilling-prone over years of wear |
| Uniqlo Merino | Lightweight (~155 gsm) | Cheapest entry; roughly a 2-year service life vs. 5+ for the others |

Pick one brand and stick to it across tee, long-sleeve, and mid-layer. Mixing brands defeats the point of a capsule built on interchangeable pieces, and it's one more decision every time you pack.

## Power system: USB-C everything

Standardize every chargeable item in the kit on USB-C input. It's a small constraint that pays off disproportionately: one cable type, no legacy-cable graveyard in the bag, and a single power bank that charges everything you own.

Check before buying: phone, laptop, headphones, e-reader, travel router, camera, any grooming electronics, and the power bank itself — confirm it both accepts and outputs USB-C PD, since some cheaper banks only do one.

**Charger.** A single high-wattage GaN brick (100W-class) can run a laptop, phone, camera, and headphones off one wall outlet simultaneously — worth the modest weight and price premium over carrying three separate chargers. Confirm the 100–240V input rating before international travel; most modern electronics are dual-voltage, but check rather than assume, especially for anything with a motor rather than a simple charging circuit.

## Network hardware

**Travel router.** A pocket-sized travel router (GL.iNet's Beryl/Slate lines are a common reference point) lets you run a VPN at the router level instead of configuring it per device, and gives every device in the room one trusted network instead of raw hotel Wi-Fi. Treat hotel and public Wi-Fi as hostile by default — a $60–100 travel router removes the recurring decision of whether to trust it.

**eSIM vs. local SIM.** For short hops, an eSIM provider (Airalo has the broadest coverage; regional providers are often cheaper for a single-country trip) beats hunting for a SIM shop on arrival. The math changes if you have a long-term base in one country: at that point a local physical SIM becomes the primary line, with eSIMs reserved for satellite trips elsewhere. Compare providers on coverage map, data allotment, and whether they support hotspot/tethering — some budget providers block it.

## Traveling with prescription medication

Carrying prescription medication across a border adds a documentation requirement a lot of travelers underestimate:

- **Keep it in original, labeled packaging.** A loose pill organizer with no packaging is the single most common reason for a customs hold.
- **Carry a physician's letter.** State the medication, dosage, and medical necessity in plain language. For destinations where English isn't the primary language, get it translated in advance rather than at the border.
- **Check destination legality before you travel, not on arrival.** Some medications that are legal at home are restricted or banned outright elsewhere, independent of whether you have a valid prescription — this is a country-by-country check, not a one-time rule.
- **Keep a copy of the documentation in two places** — physical, with the medication, and a digital copy (encrypted) accessible even if the physical copy is lost or held.
- **Some countries require an import certificate for larger supplies.** Japan's *Yakkan Shoumei* process is a well-known example — required above a certain quantity, and it has to be filed before arrival, not after.

## EDC philosophy

Minimum viable everyday carry: wallet, phone, keys, and — if you shoot photos — a camera you're actually willing to have in hand rather than left at the room. Whether a watch makes the cut is mostly a tier-compliance call (see [gray-man tier framework](../frameworks/situational_awareness.md)): unrestricted in low-threat contexts, off the wrist in higher-tier ones.

**Multi-tool or knife.** Check the destination's legal blade length before you pack one — Japan, for example, caps carried blades at 6 cm regardless of intent. A compact multi-tool covers most travel use cases (box cutting, minor repairs); going knifeless entirely is also a legitimate choice if the rest of your kit doesn't otherwise need one.

**Pen and notebook.** Most travelers now cover this with a phone note app — one less thing to carry, one less thing to lose. Worth explicitly deciding rather than defaulting to "bring a notebook" out of habit.

## Camera

**Compact fixed-lens vs. phone or DSLR.** If you shoot enough to want a dedicated camera, a compact fixed-lens body (the Fujifilm X100-series and Ricoh GR-series are the common reference points) beats a DSLR-plus-lenses setup for travel on every axis that matters here: roughly a third of the size and weight, no lens-swapping behavior that flags "photographer at work," and — because it doesn't look like a large DSLR — most non-photographers simply don't register it as expensive gear. A single fixed lens also forces a shooting discipline (compose with your feet, not a zoom ring) that a lot of photographers end up preferring anyway.

**Buy for the repair network, not just the spec sheet.** Check where the brand's service centers sit relative to where you'll actually be — a camera with no realistic repair path near your home base is a camera you'll eventually replace instead of fix. Sensor and lens are typically non-serviceable on a fixed-lens body, but the body itself is usually rated for 5–7 years of regular use before it needs service.

**Card discipline.** Whatever you shoot on, back it up before it becomes a single point of failure: copy cards to a portable drive nightly, upload the important frames to cloud storage when you have Wi-Fi, and store the backup drive separately from the camera. If the camera gets lost or stolen, the work should survive it.
